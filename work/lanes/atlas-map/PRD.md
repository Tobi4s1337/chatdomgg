## The part
The Atlas becomes the map of Twitch you would show anyone, and the front door of Chatdom stands on it.

Open chatdom.io/map, or scroll to the bottom of the front page, and you see every Twitch community as land: continents by the language people stream in, named plainly and carefully ("Streams in Russian", "Cantonese"), realms by what they play, a vermilion wash where people are live right now, and ink roads where one community has raided another. Above it sits one carved brass-and-wood cartouche with the wordmark and the search, and a pictorial key of about five glyphs. No paragraphs. Type any Twitch name, even a two-viewer channel that went offline an hour ago, and the camera flies to its place, where a card with the streamer's own face in a wax-seal frame tells you whether they are live and what they play, with a way to watch on Twitch. Nobody can tell which channels the survey saw and which ones the service just asked Twitch about.

Roads are memory. Every raid Chatdom witnesses inks a road between two kingdoms that survives restarts, thickens with use, crosses the sea between languages as a sea lane, fades to a dotted line when unused and never disappears. At the end of a stream the monarch opens the Raid Gate and the same map opens in gate mode: live friends along existing roads first, then small live neighbours, smaller ones before bigger ones, never anything paid.

The land stops moving. Edition I is founded from a week of real observation and Tobias's signature, and from then on nobody's place changes; new channels slot into free land.

It is one map in three modes, exported as a clean embed: `hero` for the front page (alive, quiet, never in the way of scrolling), `explore` for the full Atlas, and `gate` for the Raid Gate. It is beautiful and smooth on a real GPU, and on a phone it is designed, not shrunk.

The bar: a still of the Atlas sits beside the best fantasy-map game UI without embarrassment; every word on screen earns its place; search never says "not here" for a name that exists on Twitch; the first map frame arrives in under 1.5 s on a laptop. You own it end to end and make the design calls; write down why in Decisions.

Use an app access token, cached until it expires. Read the `Ratelimit-Remaining` and `Ratelimit-Reset` headers on every reply: the Atlas fallback spends at most about 60 points a minute and stops asking (survey answers only, "try again in a moment" for the rest) whenever fewer than 200 points remain, so the kingdoms and the survey never starve. Back off on 429 and 5xx with jitter, never in a storm. Tests mock Twitch; real lookups are only for evidence and stay to a handful. The Atlas never writes to Twitch.

After the merge, adopting your code on the live service (a rebuild, a restart with the Twitch env file, a fresh preview with the new names, the gateway routes, an edition) is an operator step: write it as a short runbook in this folder and a `needs:` line.

Always the full URL. Write the numbers in progress. A real phone's numbers are Tobias's to take: write `needs: phone check <path>`.
